Route Overview

East Buttress @ Mt. Whitney

Mt. Whitney is supposedly the highest point in the lower 48, meaning the highest point in the US outside Alaska and Hawaii (Hawaii doesn't have any really high mountains, so basically the highest outside Alaska), and is also the 11th-highest peak in the US [Wikipedia]. The standard way up starts from Whitney Portal, follows the Mount Whitney Trail, and joins Mt. Whitney's south ridge (this section is also the southernmost part of the John Muir Trail, or JMT) to the summit. Since this route is relatively easy, it is very popular outside snow season. There is a limited permit quota, obtained through a lottery system.

Besides the Mount Whitney Trail, another way to summit is via the North Fork of Lone Pine Creek Trail, also starting from Whitney Portal and going directly to Iceberg Lake below Mt. Whitney's east face. From there, there are two options. One is the Mountaineer's Route, which climbs the snow couloir on the north side (in summer the snow melts out, leaving a loose gully that becomes 3rd/4th class) to the summit. The other is to climb any of the 5th-class routes, the most popular being the East Buttress, an 11-pitch 5.7 alpine route. After climbing the East Buttress, the Mountaineer's Route is normally used for the descent.

To climb the East Buttress, first drive to Whitney Portal (~h2544m). If you're flying in, you can fly to either LAX or LAS; both are international airports and both are about a 4.5-hour drive from the Portal. Then hike in to Iceberg Lake (~h3850m). Most people camp there and start climbing early the next morning, though some camp lower at Upper Boy Scout Lake (~h3460m). The next day, approach the route start (roughly ~h4100m), then climb 11 pitches to the summit (h4421m). The Mountaineer's Route descent has two parts: summit to "The Notch," then The Notch to Iceberg Lake. The Notch is a small saddle connecting the east and west sides. Above the Notch there are two ways down: one is 3rd class lower down and 4th class higher up, with the steepest final 400 feet commonly called the Final 400'. The other is the "Easy walkoff," which traverses farther west to easier terrain (see the fourth image below).

Our rack: Full double rack with nuts and tricams, except single #4. No ball nuts. We did use the #4, but not the TCUs.

Snake Dike @ Half Dome

There are two especially famous rocks in Yosemite Valley: El Capitan (the one Alex Honnald free soloed) and Half Dome. On the south face of Half Dome is a famous climbing route called Snake Dike, rated 5.7 R. The R means runout: the route has 8 pitches, and P3 through P6 each contain sections with an R rating. For example, P4 is about 160' (48 m) with only one bolt in the middle, though that section is only 5.4. People have argued for years over whether more bolts should be added, without ever reaching a consensus. Then in June 2026, someone actually went and retrobolted it, causing a huge uproar, and all the added bolts were chopped within a week. The arguments behind the controversy are complicated; see the two original threads:ref1, ref2, plus these takes that I thought were pretty fair:1, 2, 3, 4, 5 (and the OP's comments) (I also think this route should really be X-rated).

To climb the route, start from Happy Isles in Yosemite Valley, the northern end of the JMT. Take the Mist Trail past Vernal Fall, rejoin the JMT, then cut onto a climber's trail to the start of the route on Half Dome's southwest face. The whole approach takes about 3-5 hours. After 8 pitches of 5th-class climbing, there is another 300+ m of 3rd-class slab to the summit.

The descent follows the standard Half Dome hiking route, The Cables, down the line of handrails installed on Half Dome's northeast face (apparently going up this way also requires a lottery permit). From there it's a much longer trail loop back to the JMT and eventually the approach route.

Our rack: Single rack of #.3-#1, 6 draws. Nothing else.

Logistics and other miscellaneous information

Permit

Entering North Fork of Lone Pine Creek Trail  and camping overnight at Cathedral Lake  both require Wilderness permits, both through Recreation.gov . They are released at 07:00 California time two weeks and one week in advance, respectively. From testing, we found that the actual release happened about 37 seconds early (i.e. 06:59:23, based on https://time.is/ ). Both are very hard to get; if you wait until 07:00 to refresh, you're definitely too late. I strongly recommend practicing a few days beforehand so you're familiar with the system before the real attempt. Also, I never ran into a CAPTCHA for either one. If you're interested, the keyword to look up is ChromeDriver.

Note: if you summit Whitney and exit via Trail Crest, there is a separate overall quota. If you plan to exit that way, choose "Overnight exiting Mt. Whitney" as the permit type.

Transportation

Mt. Whitney is actually about a 4.5-hour drive from either LAX or LAS, but since we both wanted to visit friends in LA anyway, we decided to fly into LAX and rent a car there. Later we added Yosemite to the plan, so after Mt. Whitney we drove straight to Yosemite, then on the last day drove six hours all the way back to LAX.

Campgrounds

Whitney Trailhead Campground

A first-come-first-serve campground at Whitney Portal. All the other campgrounds are insanely hard to reserve, so unless you start planning very early, this is probably the only realistic option for most people.

  • Location: drive all the way to the end at the Portal and loop around (it's one-way there). Park on the opposite, south side of the Portal; the campsites are along the road. The two signs above are roughly here.
  • Reservation: first-come-first-serve. Before the trip, Reddit had us convinced there was no way we'd get a spot arriving late on a Saturday, but when we got there there were still quite a few open spaces. It looks like there are designated tent pads, basically flattened patches of ground, though some are a little sloped. There aren't site numbers or anything.
  • Facilities: bear lockers in the parking area and a vault toilet. I didn't see a faucet. No cell service.

Iceberg Lake (dispersed)

A natural camping option for climbing Mt. Whitney or Mt. Russel.

  • Location: south side of Iceberg Lake (36.58033, -118.28514). There are several obvious prepared tent sites, some with stone windbreaks.
  • Reservation: none
  • Facilities: dispersed camping, so naturally there is nothing except water. Bring your own bear can. Human waste cannot be buried; you need a Wag bag and have to pack it out (free bags are available at the Portal, which also has a disposal point). For water, bring a filter and use lake water. Obviously no cell service either (though there is service on the summit of Mt. Whitney).

Cathedral Lake (dispersed)

Since the Southeast Buttress on Cathedral Peak is extremely popular, we decided, based on a few YouTube videos, to hike in the night before and camp by Cathedral Lake. As far as we could tell, we were the only climbers camping there that night; some of the others looked like they were hiking the JMT.

  • Location: around Upper Cathedral Lake. We camped on the east side, where there were plenty of open spots, though it looked like quite a few people were camping on the west side too.
  • Reservation: none, but camping here overnight requires a Wilderness permit (see Permit), and you can't camp too close to the trail or water sources (the ranger will explain this when you pick up the permit).
  • Facilities: since it's dispersed camping, naturally there is nothing except water. Bring your own bear can. You don't have to pack out human waste; you can dig a cathole (the ranger will tell you how deep, and ask whether you brought a trowel). For water, bring a filter and use lake water. No cell service.

Tuolumne Meadows Backpacker Campground

Campground entrance

Rules posted at the campground entrance

At the campground entrance: a small envelope where you put cash/fill in payment info and drop it in the box next to it, plus a tag to leave at your campsite

Yosemite's backpacker campgrounds are for people with Wilderness permits to camp the night before and the night after their wilderness trip. See the official page for the detailed rules. The sign here says it's only for people without cars, but that seems to be an outdated rule because the website doesn't say that. When we drove in, the host only told us there was no overnight parking inside, just temporary parking for loading/unloading.

  • Location: drive all the way to the back of Tuolumne Meadows Campground (roughly here). There is a booth at the campground entrance where you can pay when you drive in, and the host will tell you where to go. Inside, there are numbered designated campsites.
  • Reservation: first-come-first-serve.
  • Parking: there is no overnight parking for backpackers inside the campground. The host will tell you to unload in the loading area, then park outside and walk back in (the closest place is at Lembert Dome). Next to the restroom there is one accessible parking space and one regular space. The regular one has no special signage, so I have no idea whether overnight parking is allowed there.
  • Facilities: every campsite has a bear locker, and there is a restroom with running water by the unloading area. There is no cell service anywhere in the Tuolumne area, including the Visitor Center.

Yosemite Valley Camp 4

Probably the most climber-friendly campground in the Valley. It's not as brutally hard to get as Upper Pines (though it's still hard). The campground is divided into numbered areas, each with roughly three or four tents sharing a bear locker and table. There are boulders all around, including the famous Midnight Lightning. Walking around, you'll see plenty of people sorting gear or carrying crash pads.

  • Location: Camp 4 Yosemite Valley.
  • Reservation: book one week ahead through Rec.gov, see official page. Even on weekdays, with over a hundred spots, they still disappear within a few seconds of release (though at least not within one second), so timing matters. If you try to automate it with a script, you'll run into CAPTCHA.
  • Parking: there is a shared parking lot, but you need a reservation to park there. You get a tag at check-in.
  • Facilities: every campsite has a bear locker and there is a restroom with running water. There is Wi-Fi, but you can mostly only connect near the check-in booth. At our site I could get LTE (on Mint), barely usable.

Groceries and gas

Besides the Walmart stop in LA at the start, after coming down from Whitney we also stopped at the Vons in Bishop, basically the only larger town on the way (Vons). After leaving Tuolumne and entering the Valley, the Valley Store actually has a decent selection of fresh food. It's a tourist area so of course it's a little expensive, but at least you can buy meat that isn't canned, like bacon, ground meat, regular chicken, etc., plus quite a few vegetables. The Valley Lodge across from Camp 4 (Yosemite Valley Lodge Gift Shop) also sells some frozen food, but the selection is much smaller.

California gas is famously expensive. After leaving LA, we topped up a little before Whitney but didn't want to fill the tank because gas in touristy Lone Pine is more expensive. After leaving there and before Yosemite, there is one gas station that is noticeably cheaper (Fort Independence Travel Plaza & Winnedumah Winns Casino). There is also a gas station at the Tioga Pass entrance (Mobil), but it is insanely expensive. Before entering the Valley there is another station that is surprisingly cheap (Crane Flat Gas Station - (24/7 Gas Pumps)). Of course, none of these beats Costco once you're back in town.

Weather

Although this area has a Mediterranean climate, the mountains can still get plenty of thunderstorms in summer. Besides rain making the rock slippery, lightning is another major hazard, and there are lightning warning signs on the various summits. Check the forecast before leaving, of course. If you're going to spend a while in areas without service (Whitney and Tuolumne), you can get weather forecasts through an InReach (apparently about $0.50 each), and the Wilderness Center and Visitor Center also provide weather information.

Also, if the wall isn't in the sun, the wind can still make it pretty cold, so remember to bring a shell. (Sneaky recommendation for the rain shell, pants, and base layer).

Bears

Warning: every area we passed through on this trip has bears. At night, all food, plus anything with a scent such as toothpaste, bug spray, sunscreen, etc., has to go in a bear locker or bear can stored away from the tent. You should also avoid leaving food smells in the car (which is why we basically never got takeout before heading up into the mountains). Otherwise, besides getting fined, a bear may literally break into and wreck your car.

See: Information page from Inyo NF and Yosemite NP.

GPS

The phone GPS and track logs were extremely useful on this trip, and honestly essential. Most places still have some sort of use trail or cairns, but where the route is faint it's easy to lose it, and getting back onto it can cost a lot of extra time and energy. In other words, someone experienced at route-finding could probably still figure things out, but having GPS and a track to follow saves a huge amount of effort. One important difference from route-finding in Taiwan is that US trails often cross talus, slabs, and similar terrain where there is simply no visible trail at all. Then you're relying entirely on cairns, and whenever there aren't any cairns, that's exactly where it's easiest to lose the route. GPS tracks are incredibly helpful in those spots.

For this trip I made my maps with CalTopo. It's very easy to use and already has useful base maps and trails. If you want to make your own, USGS publishes maps openly (see How to download maps from the USGS database: Mt. Whitney as an example), but I still haven't found a base map that's as convenient as Taiwan's Lu Maps and can just be imported directly (if anyone knows one, please tell me). CalTopo can export GeoPDF directly. The image quality is a little worse, but still plenty usable.

My phone is a Pixel 10. I left Extreme Battery Saver on for the entire week in California, and the battery lasted way longer than I expected: more than three days on a full charge. I never needed a power bank and could just recharge while driving. The app I use is OruxMaps. There are some annoying issues on newer versions of Android; see Using offline maps in OruxMaps.

SAR information

If you're calling from the scene yourself, just call 911. If the home contact is the one calling, though, apparently 911 centers in different areas may not be able to transfer calls between each other or something along those lines, so use the numbers below:

  • Inyo county dispatch (for Mt. Whitney): (760) 878-0383
  • Yosemite dispatch: (209) 379-1992

T-Mobile has a T-satellite service that supports text messages and text-to-911. You can subscribe for just one month and cancel afterward. We didn't use it, but it looks great.

HC

First, thanks to PC for being our home contact, D for providing a lot of useful information when consulted by PC, and my sister for lending us the bear can and water filter. Most importantly, thanks again to PH, my rope partner in suffering. At this point, in the US at least, I don't think there is a second person besides PH whom I'd trust enough to climb routes like these with.

There was a lot of walking on this trip, hiking in and hiking out, which inevitably gave me a lot of time to think. This is my sixth year climbing now. I only started in the first place because the mountaineering club wouldn't take me. Trad went from unfamiliar, to terrifying, to something I finally started getting used to once I owned a complete double rack I knew well. Only over the last six months or so have I slowly started building some confidence on the wall, instead of getting on every route wondering whether I'll finish it or whether I'll be able to get all my gear back. Between J-Tree at the end of last year and Whitney on this trip, I finally feel like I'm starting to relax and actually enjoy being on the wall. The nerves are definitely still there, but now there also seems to be this excitement that's hard to suppress.

The first time I'd heard of Mt. Whitney was actually because my sister won a permit lottery to climb it. At the time I thought it was just hiking on trails and wasn't especially interested. (They actually went in early summer when there was still plenty of snow, so it wasn't quite that simple.) Then later I randomly saw an Instagram video of someone climbing East Buttress and got immediately hooked by the scenery. A quick Google search said 5.7, 11 pitches. Purely in terms of climbing difficulty, I knew that shouldn't be too much of a problem, but it was alpine climbing: a long route, in remote mountains. Before this, the longest route I'd climbed in the US was probably Dark Shadows in Red Rocks (5.8, 4 pitches). I knew this was not remotely as simple as the grade made it sound, so I tagged PH and asked whether he thought it was viable.

The comment that sparked the trip.

Having PH as a climbing partner in the US has been incredibly fortunate. I probably have an advantage over him on a lot of moves because I'm taller with longer arms, but when it comes to trad experience, or outdoor experience in general, I'm nowhere near him. Because I don't have as much experience, I'm often not confident making decisions at the crag, and PH is usually the one who can give a calm, solid call. For example, when we were planning the complicated descent from Cathedral Peak, having PH there made me much more confident that we'd be able to improvise on the wall than I would have been by myself. PH is also one of those people on a trad route where I can think, "If I can't finish it, whatever, he'll go up and retrieve my gear." (That actually happened at Red Rocks two years ago; dig up the old report if you're interested.) This trip only started becoming a real plan after I sent him the video and he also thought it looked doable. Otherwise I definitely would have watched the video and left it at that XD. Later in the trip, he also dragged himself through Snake Dike with me while sick and still exhausted from Mt. Whitney, which left me both guilty and very grateful.

I actually wasn't interested in Yosemite at first, because the most famous thing is El Cap and without big-wall skills there was no chance I was climbing that. Only after PH suggested Yosemite did I browse Mountain Project and see the tower on the Cathedral Peak route (I didn't learn until we seriously started route planning that the tower in that photo isn't actually Cathedral Peak but the neighboring Eichorn Pinnacle) and DAFF Dome. They were types of terrain I'd never climbed before, which inevitably made me a little interested. Snake Dike was also PH's idea. At first I saw the R rating and immediately backed off; zero interest. It wasn't until the Snake Dike drama happened later that I agreed maybe we'd try it if the new bolts were still there when we arrived. While preparing, I watched a lot of videos, and eventually the idea of climbing Half Dome got me too. The problem is that GoPro footage on YouTube makes it very hard to tell how steep or exposed something actually is. The bolts were chopped in less than a week anyway, but slowly I started thinking, "I've heard there's only been one serious accident in decades. It's 5.7. I should be fine, right?" And just like that, Snake Dike ended up on the objective list.

East Buttress may only be 5.7, but hiking in with a heavy pack and gaining 1,300 m is no joke. I also hadn't backpacked with serious weight for many years, so for about a month before the trip I dug out the physical-training standards we used back in my university mountaineering club and tried to get back in shape. Unfortunately my little town doesn't have any tall buildings to climb with weight. And as someone who doesn't go to the gym, suddenly walking into one with a 30 kg backpack just to use the stair machine felt way too embarrassing, so I ran instead. Fortunately my cardio didn't seem to have declined much, so I just prayed adding the pack wouldn't make everything immediately fall apart. In reality, I was definitely breathing hard on the hike in, but the glacial terrain along the way was unlike anything I'd seen in person, and the hike felt far less miserable than I'd expected. The mix of rock and vegetation around Lower Boy Scout Lake, the slabby stream and little cascades near Upper Boy Scout, the U-shaped valley before Iceberg Lake, and Iceberg Lake itself honestly displaced every mountain I've climbed in Taiwan in my personal ranking. (Not that I've climbed that many mountains in Taiwan.) On the way down, it was all scenery we'd already seen on the ascent, but sunrise made everything feel different. We just kept hiking along looking at the views and suddenly we were back at the Portal.

On East Buttress itself, because we were worried about thunderstorms and still hadn't completely given up on hiking all the way out that same day, I basically had two modes. While PH was climbing, I looked at the scenery, took photos, rested, and studied the topo for my next lead. When it was my turn to climb, I just focused on one move after another. While leading I deliberately ran things out a little more to save time; fortunately the climbing was easy enough that this wasn't too scary. We were in a bit of a hurry, but once the faster party behind us passed there wasn't much pressure anymore. By the later pitches we knew hiking out that day was hopeless, but at least the climbing itself stayed smooth and we didn't spend too much time sitting around on the wall. If I climbed another route of similar difficulty, though, I might use more simul-climbing for efficiency. Afterward it was obvious that a large fraction of this route could be simuld. I just didn't realize until after leading P8 that the rest probably made sense to simul, and then remembered that I barely have any simul experience beyond the most basic operation. I couldn't even remember the last time I'd taken in a Kiwi coil. Next time on a low-difficulty route with reliable protection, I should probably be more practiced at simul techniques. I happened to find these videos afterward, plus Brent's article, which may be worth checking out).

The Mountaineer's Route descent was probably the most miserable part of the whole trip for me. I have very little experience with this kind of 2nd/3rd-class scrambling (not even 4th class!), and the whole way down I didn't even have spare mental bandwidth to question my life choices. I was completely tense, carefully placing every step and thinking that if this foot slipped I'd slide all the way to the bottom of the mountain. PH ahead of me looked totally unbothered, and I kept thinking about all the day hikers we'd passed coming up the day before. Do none of these people get scared? When I was learning to lead and practicing how to deal with fear of falling, I learned about the fight-or-flight response. I have a feeling that after this experience I may actually be more afraid the next time I encounter this kind of 3rd- or even 4th-class scrambling, especially loose scree. Maybe I won't be interested in this kind of alpine route anymore. But who knows. Like PH said, maybe after enough time passes I'll see some new route and get tempted again, haha.

The Southeast Buttress on Cathedral Peak was a completely different experience. Because of the forecast we'd seen before starting, I was thinking the whole time that we were racing the weather and needed to get back down as early as possible to reduce thunderstorm risk. The result was that I climbed tense and rushed. By the time I reached the planned anchor my morale was already mostly gone, and then it started raining. Continuing upward just seemed too risky, so this became my first time bailing from a multipitch route. If we did it again, I think we'd still choose to start that morning and we'd still choose to bail once it rained. I was disappointed not to finish the route, but I think we both agreed the decisions we made at the time were right, and neither of us regrets them at all. We can only blame Tuolumne (tu-O-lou-me!) weather for being so unpredictable. On the plus side, I got one real bail under my belt, and now I have a reason to come back.

Snake Dike was yet another completely new experience. I've had moments in climbing before where falling wasn't an option, but just thinking about a runout of 20 still makes my palms sweat. Before this trip I actually felt pretty good about my granite friction-slab experience, but Cathedral Lake had completely wrecked that confidence. For the several hours hiking out from Cathedral Lake, I kept doubting myself: if I got that nervous on 5.6, could I really do 5.7 on Snake Dike? But then I thought about it and realized I was basically as ready as I ever will be. My confidence on 5.7 slab probably wasn't going to change much no matter how much more I climbed. The problem that morning seemed to be that I was too rushed to calm down and actually find each move. By the time we reached the trailhead, I'd convinced myself that if I could stay calm and find the moves one step at a time, it should be fine. To avoid repeating the same mistake and having P1 trad destroy my confidence again, I shamelessly asked PH if he could take P1 and P2. Fortunately he agreed immediately. Once I reached the P2 anchor, I found that the first bolt on P3 was nowhere near as far away as I'd imagined, the angle wasn't as terrifying as expected, and the crux after the bolt looked like maybe two moves. It looked doable, so I went. The dike was pretty much what I'd expected: the runouts were huge, but if you simply don't look down, focus on the holds in front of you, and make sure every move is stable and confident, the pitch is over surprisingly quickly. After climbing it, I can understand ("I don't condone, I understand!") why people oppose adding bolts: it's a historic route, and if you climb carefully, the chance of actually falling on the runout pitches is genuinely low. I would still like to see a few more bolts on the P1 alt-start and P3-6 to make the route safer for future climbers. A great route shouldn't be gatekept; it should be something everyone can share.

It's been two weeks since I got home now. Looking through the photos, the scenery of Yosemite Valley and the jagged peaks around Whitney are still incredibly vivid. I have mixed feelings about alpine climbing. On one hand, I'm drawn to the mountain scenery and the freedom that only climbers get to experience on those walls. On the other, I'm scared of basically any 3rd/4th-class scrambling. It may be hard to convince myself to take the next step again anytime soon, but there's no guarantee I won't see some new route in the future and get tempted by the mountains all over again. As John Muir said: "The mountains are calling and I must go."

As my climbing experience and skills gradually accumulated, I realized that climbing itself is another way to get close to a mountain, and it lets you see scenery that belongs only to climbers.

Sometime around March or April, I watched Cody's Instagram video of alpine climbing on Whitney and immediately got itchy. Perfect clean granite with alpine lakes behind the climbers: I think anyone who likes multipitch climbing and nice scenery would have a hard time resisting. Even though my last experience on The Diamond at Longs Peak was absolutely miserable, part of me still missed that feeling of freedom from being high on a mountain wall. So HC and I started planning to climb Mt. Whitney via the East Buttress. Mt. Whitney in late summer is much more approachable: you only have to deal with rock, without the more complicated mix of ice, snow, and rock. Of course we weren't going to fly all the way to California for just one climb. I also had no idea how much longer I'd be in the US, so we added Yosemite while we were at it.

Before the trip I already knew the approach would be a major challenge for me. I hadn't been in the high mountains for a long time, and carrying heavy loads has never been one of my strengths. I worked pretty hard for about a month beforehand on Zone 2 and weighted training, but my performance at altitude still wasn't great. Around Upper Boy Scout Lake I could clearly feel the fatigue and weakness. It had been a long time since I'd needed actual willpower just to take the next step. I found myself gritting my teeth, silently counting breaths with each step, pushing upward like I was back in my first year of college just starting to hike big mountains. The difference was that this pack was heavier because of all the climbing gear, and there was more elevation gain. Fortunately HC took some of my gear and patiently moved at my pace. We'd originally even fantasized about reaching camp early enough to scout the next day's approach. As we got close to Iceberg Lake, the Crook, Keeler, and Whitney ridge suddenly loomed right in front of us. That jagged chain of steep peaks always makes me think of photos from Patagonia. Taiwan's high mountains and the Colorado 14ers I'd climbed before had never given me this kind of shock. They looked like a row of giant lower fangs lined up in front of us. We were only climbing along the gentler, weaker parts of the ridgeline; imagining climbing the other steep walls was beyond me. All I could do was stare.

The next morning we followed other parties to the route start. P1 already felt a little harder than I expected. Partly I hadn't trad climbed in a while, and partly my body wasn't used to hard exercise at high altitude. It felt like my athletic ability had been marked down to 75% like convenience-store food near its expiration date. Every gear placement and every clip felt more tiring than it would down low, and coiling the rope after the pitch drained the last bit of strength from my arms. P3 and P4 were very runout, but fortunately the moves were easy. Looking back, I actually think P1 felt like the hardest pitch of the whole route. By P6 I'd gone numb to long runouts. The protection started to feel like decoration, or maybe just something to show the follower where to go. To be honest, I wasn't obsessively perfecting every placement or anchor either. If it felt 70/100 good, that was good enough; I didn't want to spend extra time tweaking it just to make it prettier. Safe, functional, and fast was enough. After P9 and some weird simul-climbing, somehow we just muddled our way to the summit. More than the joy of reaching the peak, I liked watching Iceberg Lake get smaller and smaller below us. I also remember following P7 and P8 thinking, how the hell did HC dare lead these weird pitches? I'd read the guidebook too and still couldn't tell what it was saying. I suddenly understood how much route-finding intuition it takes to know where to go. And where did he get the courage to just commit to that chimney-over-whatever terrain?

The next day hiking down, my legs no longer felt like mine. There wasn't a single leg muscle that wasn't sore, and I was starting to develop some cold symptoms. Then we moved on to Yosemite, where the next objective was Cathedral Peak (I call it Cat Peak). Cat Peak is a really interesting-looking mountain. The old line about how a mountain looks like a ridge from one angle and a peak from another, changing with distance and height, fits it perfectly. Personally I think it looks like a cat with two pointed ears, or maybe Batman's head. We had to bail because of rain and never got to stand on its head, but the scenery at Cat Lake (Cathedral Lake) and Cat Peak alone was worth hiking in for. I genuinely didn't feel like the bailout was much of a loss. Ignoring the unfinished climbing, it was basically a five-star lakeside camping experience. Naturally, after we hiked out the weather cleared. Was that frustrating? Sure. But at the time we'd made the most rational and appropriate decision we could. The rest was luck.

Another day later, my cold had gotten worse. My steps felt floaty, I was dizzy and chilled. It wasn't severe, but it was definitely not a body condition suited for climbing and hiking. We canceled West Crack that day and went into Yosemite early to wander around like normal tourists. Personally, I wasn't disappointed about canceling at all, though I did feel a little bad for HC because he clearly had plenty of energy and wanted to climb. That rest day was too important for me. If we'd climbed, it would have been equivalent to taking no rest day before the final boss, Snake Dike, and I probably would have died. In hindsight it was the right call. Camp 4 was also terrible for sleeping that night: people talking after midnight, kids screaming and playing, traffic noise. It was hard to get to sleep early.

We got up at three the next morning. I wasn't in perfect condition, but I was dramatically better than the day before. Only when we reached the wall did I realize how different the slab angle looked in person compared with videos and photos. P1 immediately taught me a lesson. I've never been good at friction slab, and once I ran it out after the first piece I was way too unsettled to tell whether my feet were actually sticking. I flailed my way to the crux on P2, discovered I'd brought the wrong cam size, and mentally collapsed all over again. Then the rope drag got awful and my hand slipped; I nearly came off in the no-fall zone. It reminded me of how ugly my first friction climbing in Squamish was. By the time I clipped the P2 anchor, half my soul was already dead. It felt like that drained crash after burning through too much adrenaline. I had seriously underestimated the first two pitches. Fortunately HC led the friction crux on P3, so I didn't have to deal with it. Having a slab-master rope partner is basically cheating.

My turns later were P4 and P6, both on raised dikes. Thinking about it afterward, having one bolt on a ~40 m pitch is kind of insane. Fortunately the climbing isn't very hard; it just burns your calves. I really liked HC's pep talk here: "Focus on the move in front of you, and before you know it the anchor will be right there." Before climbing, all kinds of internal and external noise was filling my head: dry mouth, strong wind, baking sun with no shade, every muscle in my legs hurting, where the hell is the bolt and why are there so few and why are they so far apart, where is the anchor and am I going to accidentally miss it, holy shit if I fall here I might die, and so on... But once I grabbed those irregular bumps on the dike, all of that disappeared. Muscle memory took over and my body automatically found the best way upward. Suddenly the finish was right there. It felt like traversing in the gym: low intensity, extremely high focus, a kind of flow state. Then friction slab came back on P7 and once again I was very glad it wasn't my lead. I basically watched on my knees as HC performed some terrifying mystery-faith friction climbing. The guidebook clearly says to go right, and he just marched straight up the featureless smooth slab on the left... All I could do was admire his mental game. P8 actually wasn't hard, and since there was protection available I really didn't want to run it out anymore, but we didn't have the right gear left and had to split it into two parts. After that came calf-burning slab hell all the way to the top of Half Dome.

From the summit of Half Dome you can see almost all of Yosemite. Looking toward Tuolumne Meadows, I suddenly realized just how beautiful Yosemite really is, and I could understand why so many legendary climbers are willing to spend years here. I was already thinking that if I ever get the chance, I definitely want to come back, whether just for climbing or even hiking. Then came the long way back. After 15 hours of climbing and hiking, I can't even remember how I squeezed out enough energy to cook dinner and then somehow put the food into my stomach. I only remember that I slept better that night than on any of the previous seven.

I'm finishing these thoughts two weeks after getting back to Virginia. If you asked me right now whether I want to do another alpine route or similar multipitch, I would reject the idea instantly and without hesitation. I'm not confident the answer will still be the same six months or a year from now, though. Grand Teton, RMNP, the unfinished Cat Peak... the US has way too much tempting scenery. Finally, thanks to my good rope partner HC: always leading the pitches I didn't dare lead, unfortunately getting my cold from me, taking gear out of my pack and waiting for me on the uphill. I still don't entirely understand the appeal of drinking soup for dinner, but I always secretly learn a little something from him, like how angel hair cooks ridiculously fast and saves fuel. Looking forward to climbing together again in the US. Alpine, though... really, not again for a while.

How to download maps from the USGS database: Mt. Whitney as an example

  • Downloaded from the USGS database. It's probably easiest to download GeoPDF files, which can be either printed out, or imported into mobile GPS apps like OruxMaps.
  • Instructions to use USGS database:
  • Go to USGS database, open the National Map Downloader, and find the trail in the map first.
  • On the top left, use any of the tools, e.g., "Extent", under "Area of interest" to select the extent of the map needed, such as the whole trail.
  • For GeoPDF, go to "Map", check "US Topo". In the subcategories, choose "US Topo current", which are the currently maintained maps.
  • For data extent, choose "7.5 x 7.5 minute" (1 minute = 1/60 degree in latitude/longitude). For file formats, choose "Geospatial PDF". Both of which are actually the only options.
  • Under "Advanced search", click "Search Product".
  • The available maps will then show up. The two maps that cover Mt. Whitney trails are "Mount Langley, CA" and "Mount Whitney, CA".
  • Click "Download Link (PDF)" to download.
  • See Using offline maps in OruxMaps for tutorials on how to put this map in OruxMaps.

Using offline maps in OruxMaps

Here, I document the steps to import geoPDFs (and possibly other formats) into OruxMaps. This is extremely useful for route finding. The GeoPDF can come from the USGS database, as described above, downloaded from CalTopo, or other custom sources.

I struggled a bit with this because there seems to be a bug in the setting of OruxMaps when selecting the folder that contains the maps, and Android 14 or newer there are some permission BS that makes it difficult to access the default directory where the app searches for the maps. One of the workarounds is to use ADB (android debug bridge). Here I document the steps that work for me.

  1. Save the downloaded PDF files in your phone internal storage, anywhere you can find it.
  2. In OruxMaps, go to settings (the gear icon inside the list on the top left), go to "App Storage". Under "Directories", look for "Maps directory". This is the default directory to put the GeoPDF files you just downloaded. Unfortunately, this is most likely under ~/Android/data/. On newer OS, permission to access this directory is banned.
  3. You can try the option "Alternative maps directory" just underneath. Try selecting a folder you can access in your phone. The detailed steps depend on the OS, but for me I couldn't select any accessible folder due to some BS about permission and an apparent bug. My only resolution is then to access the default directory with ADB.
  4. Install ADB on your PC (E.g., for macOS, see here).
  5. Enable ADB on your phone (It is under developer options. Detailed instructions depend on the phone), and connect it to the PC. Run "adb devices" from your CLI. If everything works out, you should see a message saying that ADB has started.
  6. As described here, run "adb shell". You now have access to the shell, and you can use the standard Linux commands (e.g., cd, mv, ls) to move the downloaded GeoPDF files to the default directory. As a side note, if you record a GPX, you would the same method to move the exported files to a readable directory too.
  7. OruxMaps can now find those files. Go back to the main menu (where you can see the maps). Tap the maps icon to go to the page where you can select map layers.
  8. Your map should now show up under the "offline" tab. You can then select them. Unfortunately it seems that you can only show one map at a time.
  9. If you have internet access, you can also go to the "Online" tab. Under "Layers -> (US)" you can find "USGS Topo (US)", which also seems to be useful.
