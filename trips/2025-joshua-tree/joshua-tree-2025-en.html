2025 Joshua Tree Trip Report

Written by: Hao-Chien

Additional notes: PH

Basic Info

Joshua Tree National Park (Joshua Tree National Park) is in Southern California, and is one of the few well-known climbing areas in the US that stays warm enough for outdoor climbing in winter. The nearest city, Palm Springs, is about 45 minutes away, and LA is about 2.5 hours away. The park has two main entrances, west and east, connected by one main road. Each day we just entered from whichever side was closer to where we wanted to climb.

People: Hao-Chien, PH, Y

Dates: 2025/12/21-31 = D0-D10. D0 and D10 were travel days.

Map: link

Weather

During the day when we were climbing, temperatures were around 5-10°C, but it was windy. Being sheltered from the wind and being in the sun made a huge difference to how cold it actually felt. So definitely bring enough warm clothes, and when picking routes it is worth checking when they get sun (the guidebook tells you).

As for rain, winter is actually not that dry statistically. Out of our 10 climbing days, it rained on three of them (D2, D3, D10). Also worth noting: on D2 the forecast looked completely dry, and then it absolutely poured. The good news is that J-Tree rock dries pretty fast. As long as it does not rain too hard, you can usually wait a while and climb once the rock dries. So even if rain is forecast, no need to give up immediately. People on the MP forum say anything below a 90% chance of rain is not really worth panicking about. Pick something easy to bail from; the rain may start later than forecast, or it may only sprinkle. On both days when rain was actually forecast for us (D3 and D10), we still climbed two routes each.

Transportation

This time we all flew into Palm Springs International Airport (PSP), then rented one car together. Flying into LAX and driving over is another option.

PSP airport is pretty cool. TSA and the gates are in different buildings, and you actually walk outside between them. The path to the plane is basically just an area fenced off with railings. When it rains they put umbrellas by the door, but on our flight home all the umbrellas after TSA were gone, so the two of us had to run through the rain to the gate. You also get rained on while boarding. Top left and bottom are photos Hao-Chien took on the way in; top right is what it looked like in the rain on the way home.

For the car we used Turo and got a Chevrolet Trax. If you are only driving inside the national park or around town, you do not need anything with high clearance. If you plan to stay on BLM land or similar places, though, having at least a little extra clearance is pretty necessary.

Camping / Accommodation

Since this student is broke, we camped the whole trip just like last time (except on the night of Day 3, when we mooched off Y's hotel voucher). Below are the campsites we stayed at. If you rent something bigger, e.g. a minivan, sleeping in the car might also work. Just note that overnight parking is not allowed in many places inside the park.

BLM Dispersed Camping Area for JNP

BLM Dispersed Camping Area for JNP

Free camping area. We stayed here for most of the trip. It is basically just open land where people can set up camp: no water, no electricity, but surprisingly decent cell service. When Google Maps takes you there, once you leave the pavement you can basically camp in any suitable open spot along the road. There is more space if you keep going toward the end. Since it is all public land, we were worried about stuff disappearing, so we packed up camp every morning and took everything with us.

One thing to watch out for: before you get all the way in, there are tons of spiky plants on the ground. Stepping on them hurts like hell, and they can puncture things, including tents. The thorns are thick enough to go through thinner shoes. So I would still recommend camping farther in. The minor downside is that the ground gets rockier, in which case a thicker sleeping pad is much nicer.

C0

The plant that punctured PH's shoe: puncturevine

Very windy on the morning of D3. From inside the tent we thought it was raining; once we went outside we realized it was sand getting blown against the tent. The wind was strong enough that the tent almost blew away while we were packing it up.

Official campsites inside the park

There are lots of campgrounds inside the park, many of them right next to climbing areas (basically the MP areas with “Campground” in the name, e.g. Hidden Valley Campground). If your goal is to climb as much as possible and waste as little time as possible, staying inside is definitely best. The downside is no water, no electricity, and no cell service. Bring everything yourself and enjoy being cut off from civilization. We did not stay at any of these this time. They seem to require booking pretty early; everything was full when we checked.

Official campsites outside the park

The same reservation system also has a few campgrounds around the park. The only one we stayed at was Black Rock Canyon Campground (Black Rock Canyon Campground). It has water and toilets, but the cell service is terrible (roughly “wait a while and maybe your message will send”), it is far from the climbing because it is outside the park, and even the ground is not very flat. Do not recommend at all.

Joshua Tree Lake

Joshua Tree Lake RV & Campground

Private campground. We stayed here for two nights (C8, C9). They charge per person, about $15/person/night. There is water, an insanely nice hot shower, and free Wi-Fi; the only thing missing is electricity. If you want to live a little more comfortably and do not mind paying or driving farther, this place is pretty good.

Food and Charging

Our routine after climbing each day was: find somewhere to sit down, rest, and discuss the next day's climbing; go to a grocery store; cook at a park (or campground); then go back to camp, set up the tent, and sleep.

Groceries

  • Walmart: Big store, basically has everything, but not much Asian food (Chinese-style noodles, for example). There are not many cheap meat options, mostly ground meat and bacon. We shopped here most of the time. They also sell cheap six-packs of eggs, which were perfect for us since we had no way to keep food cold.
  • ALDI: Basically a smaller Walmart. Fewer things overall, but slightly more meat options.
  • Grocery Outlet: Supposedly specializes in discounted groceries, but after comparing prices we found it was not actually cheaper. It does have some mysterious foreign foods though.
  • Stater Bros. Markets: Also kind of like ALDI. Slightly more expensive than Walmart, but way more meat options, including lots of fresh meat.

Parks

After buying groceries we almost always found a nearby city park to cook, because the parks here are amazing: water, lights, even outlets! So this became our almost-daily charging opportunity. If you use public facilities like this, keep the place clean and do not leave a mess behind (e.g. clogging the sink).

  • Paradise Park: Closer to the west entrance and Walmart. Downside: the restroom light seemed to be broken.

Paradise Park

Paradise Park. The outlet next to the northernmost table works (it is at a different shelter, not the one in this photo).

  • Knott Sky Park: Close to the east entrance, also has water and electricity. It seemed a little windier, the drinking fountain flow was a bit weak, and the tables are those metal mesh tables with holes in them, so be careful that your stove does not tip over.

Knott Sky Park

There are outlets next to the tables at Knott Sky Park. We used a #5 to hang our stuff there.

What We Ate

Breakfast and lunch were sandwiches, same as last year: bread with tomato, canned chicken breast, and peanut butter. Sometimes we were too lazy to actually make the sandwich and just ate everything separately. We also often climbed until we forgot to eat lunch.

For the first few days we were still seriously making actual sandwiches.

Dinner was hot food. We brought two stoves: basically one pot for noodles (pasta is easiest to buy) and one pot for soup, with everything else thrown in. Americans seem to make campfires a lot while camping. You buy firewood at a store, then start it one of a few ways: (1) giant fire starters almost as thick as the logs, (2) some kind of flammable charcoal that lights directly with a lighter (what we used), or (3) pour some sort of flammable liquid on it (also sold at Walmart). But just like in the mountains, cooking this way is slow and unreliable, so probably do not make it your main food source.

Our food usually looked like this: a pot of water with vegetables that keep reasonably well (onion, cabbage, tomato), plus whatever meat we bought that day (usually bacon or ground meat because they were cheaper). The supermarkets were closed on Christmas, so that day we used canned chicken instead.

BBQ on the night of D5. Most designated campsites in the US have fire rings like this, usually with a grill grate, but they are all super dirty. We thought aluminum foil would solve the problem, but everything stuck to it and the foil even started burning. If you actually want to grill meat, strongly recommend buying a grill grate.

Rest / Hanging Out

After our Red Rock experience last time, we again looked for McDonald's to charge stuff, wipe ourselves down, and rest. The McDonald's around here all have No Loitering signs saying you can only stay for half an hour, though if you go a bit over without being too obvious, nobody seems to care.

Both McDonald's had No Loitering signs like this.

  • West-side McDonald's: No outlets! The Google Maps reviews are terrible, but honestly it looked pretty normal. Contrary to the reviews, the men's restroom did not need a code.
  • East-side McDonald's: Has outlets, in the room farther from the counter.

Water

There is basically nowhere inside the national park to fill water, so remember to fill up outside first. Places where we found water:

  • Walmart: One of those upward-spraying drinking fountains. The stream is not very high, so at least one of our bottles could not be filled all the way.
  • City parks: Upward-spraying drinking fountains (same issue as above). The flow at Knott Sky Park is a bit weak.
  • East-side NP Visitor Center: Normal bottle-filling fountain.
  • map marks locations with water. West Entrance Station was under construction when we visited and did not seem to be open.
  • The campgrounds mentioned above that have water.

JTNP Logistics

Entrance Fee / Pass

We reused the annual pass PH bought at Red Rock last year. One thing worth noting: at least during staffed hours, they also check passes when you leave the park. So if you do not have an annual pass and are thinking about entering/leaving at weird hours to avoid paying, keep that in mind.

Visitor Center(s)

There are two: Joshua Tree Visitor Center on the west side, and Joshua Tree National Park Visitor Center. Only the east-side one has a water fountain. Both sell guidebooks. Across from the west-side visitor center there is also a gift shop (Coyote Corner) that is worth wandering into.

Climbing

Rock

Granite. The crystals are a little like Longdong but bigger, which also means the crystal hand/footholds are better. The rock feels more solid than Red Rock sandstone too, and you do not have the “do not climb after rain” issue.

Guidebook

As usual, there is Mountain Project (see the Red Rock report for more details; remember to download the photos for offline use!), plus guidebooks. The visitor centers sell guidebooks. There is also a digital guidebook, but we did not buy it (Joshua Tree Rock Climbing guidebook ).

Update: Anna's Archive has an electronic copy of the Bob Gaines guidebook (.epub). Maybe because it is EPUB, the image quality is a bit worse. This version only covers sport/trad, no bouldering. The visitor center sells the Robert Miramontes version, which includes bouldering.

Crashpad

Joshua Tree also has lots of bouldering, so we spent D9 bouldering for a full day. We rented one big pad and one small pad. That is basically only enough for easier stuff where you are unlikely to fall. If you want to seriously try problems with bigger moves, either bring 4+ pads or go to popular problems and gamble on other people being there. At False Hueco, Yabba Dabba Stone, and Caveman Boulder, we ran into at least one other group each time, so there were at least four pads total.

High gravity rentals

We rented from here. It is basically a vending-machine-style rental system. One warning: although they claim online that they almost never run out of pads, when we showed up a little after 9 there was only one big pad and one small pad left. So going early is probably a good idea.

Route Notes

Note: Joshua Tree grades are seriously sandbagged. To us they felt about 2-3 grades harder than Longdong (e.g. J-Tree 5.7 felt roughly like Longdong 5.9-5.10a).

D1 12/22

First day here, so we went to the super-popular Hidden Valley Campground area. Note that on Google Maps you want to navigate to the Intersection parking lot, because the campground itself does not connect to that parking lot. If the lot is full, then use the roadside parking in the campground.

  • Double Cross (5.7+): PH led.

Maybe not the best choice for the first route of the trip. You basically want three #3s to climb it without constantly worrying, and at the time we went up with only one...
...which meant we had to lower and retrieve the #3. Guidebooks and MP do not always give very detailed rack beta. After this, we basically brought at least doubles whenever leading. Felt like Longdong 5.9.

Hao-Chien following.

  • Dogleg (5.8+): PH led.

Very tricky start. Got thoroughly schooled by the hand crack / thin hands. Climbing without nuts was painful; we had to keep removing lower pieces. The descent was kind of fun: move over to the east side, then downclimb a chimney to find the rap rings. Felt like Longdong 5.10b.

Defeated by the thin hands, so I cheated with holds on the arete.

We were both tired after those two, so we wandered around the nearby boulders. Someone I walked past asked if I knew how this problem went, so we tried it together.

  • Brief Squeeze (V1-): A little burly, but the moves are small. One pad is enough.

Brief Squeeze. The climber in pink invited me as I walked by (and the woman in green behind us) to try it together. Start with the right hand on the pinch where the left foot goes, and left hand on the sloper to the left.

D2 12/23

We originally went to Real Hidden Valley to climb Fote Hog (5.6), but it started raining after three pieces. Luckily the beginning is a chimney and was not hard to downclimb. After that we walked the loop trail and scouted routes, stopping by areas like Illusion Dweller and Tumbling Rainbow. We eventually picked Sail Away as a possible route for the next day.

After the rain stopped we wandered around the bouldering area. A couple noticed me watching what they were climbing and said I could join them, so I ran back to the car for my shoes and tried these two problems with them.

  • Yabo Roof (V3): Juggy roof problem. According to the guidebook, maybe the world's first sit start?
  • Nitwitsit (V4): Sit-start extension to Nitwit. The start has bad handholds and terrible feet, almost feels like you have to campus it.

Nitwitsit: start from the bad holds and go to the jug on the right.

D3 12/24

Rain was forecast, so we squeezed in these two routes at Real Hidden Valley before it got too heavy. Then we drove back to Palm Springs to meet Y and conveniently stayed at his hotel.

  • Sail Away (5.8-): PH led. It rained in the morning, so the wall was wet. The first 2/3 of the route was wet, but the footholds actually had decent friction. Even wet granite sloper feet were surprisingly okay. It got drier higher up; maybe the wind dries the upper wall faster? At the end I went a bit too far right and ended up forcing the finger crack, only to realize later there were big handholds on the left... Felt like Longdong 5.10a.

Hao-Chien rappelling after Sail Away. He is right at the upper finger crack.

  • Easy Day (5.4): Jugs from bottom to top, but also rain from bottom to top. Water was literally dripping off the handholds while climbing. Probably pretty easy when dry, and very easy to protect.

Hao-Chien leading Easy Day. The wall was already wet, and water was dripping off the underclings. Felt a little like canyoning.

D4 12/25

Climbed at Split Rocks today.

  • Bumpy (5.8-): Hao-Chien led. Sport route right next to the parking lot. Very cool movement up a staircase of slopers.

Bumpy. The staircase-like slopers are cool. The first to second bolt is a little far.

The next three routes are all at Isles in the Sky. After walking the trail there is a section of 4th-class scrambling: a low-angle face with a hand crack in the middle. There are bolts at the top that you can rappel from on the way down. We left some gear at the bottom. Hao-Chien soloed up light to fix a line, and after climbing the routes we rappelled from the existing rings.

The scrambling section on the Isles in the Sky approach. I soloed the crack on the right, but the bolts are farther left, so PH was on the left face while jugging with the heavy pack. Photo is our simul rap on the way back.

  • Nectar (5.4): Hao-Chien led. Very easy, clean crack with great protection.

Nectar, a very comfortable crack.

  • Dolphin (5.7): PH led. The first half is an easy hand crack, #.75-3. The second half is the fun part: off-width (OW), needing #4-6. Basically use elbows, knees, and whatever else works to slowly worm your way upward. I cannot compare it to anything we have climbed at Longdong; maybe a little easier than 重劍無鋒 at Leiting Rock on Mt. Keelung.

PH in the upper off-width on Dolphin.

  • Bird of Fire (5.10a): Hao-Chien led. There is a short runout before the crack starts, so while rappelling the previous route we pre-placed the first #.5 at the bottom of the crack. Above is a finger crack, with an overhanging final section, though there is plenty of stuff on the side to cheat with. We brought lots of small cams, but in reality #.5-#1 got used the most. Before the upper overhang I placed a #.5 and then went straight to the top jug.

Hao-Chien leading Bird of Fire. The overhanging finger crack starts a little above here.

D5 12/26

Wanted to do some multipitch today. Y was doing roped climbing with us.

  • The Swift (5.7, 3 pitches): We had a communication failure here. Hao-Chien and Y thought we were climbing Dappled Mare, while PH thought the whole time we were climbing The Swift. P1: PH led. The route wanders all over the place, so rope drag was horrible. He kept expecting to reach rap rings and never saw them, then realized there are no bolts at all (Dappled Mare is the one with bolts). So he had actually led all of the original first pitch of The Swift and almost all of the second pitch in one go. No wonder the rope drag was terrible... We also used too much rope, leaving less than 35 m near the end, so Hao-Chien seconded the last part in simul mode. P2: Hao-Chien led. You traverse from the corner out onto the arete. There is an obvious big foothold that looks like the traverse point, but it is not; the actual move is after that. Pretty exposed. P3: PH led. There was less than 10 m left... very easy. Walk-off to the north. Some sections require awkward downclimbing.

PH leading P1 of The Swift. The bend around the chimney below created a lot of rope drag. There was originally another bend before the chimney, but PH took a straight-up shortcut that reduced it a little.

Hao-Chien leading P2. After climbing the corner for a while, you move out to the right. I did a layback on the first move out. Pretty exposed.

Then we went to Ryan Campground for some easy sport climbing. These two routes are on Headstone Rock, sitting on top of a pile of big boulders, so you have to scramble up a little first.

  • Cryptic (5.8): Hao-Chien led. Clip a few bolts on the slab at the start (a little spaced), then traverse on jugs to the arete. There are MP photos of people doing cool-looking dynos here, but you really do not need to cut feet at all.

Cryptic. The second half traverses to the arete. The entire row is jugs.

  • SW Corner (5.6): PH led. Supposedly the scariest 5.6 in the world?? Slab first, then the arete, with HUGE exposure!!! It was already dark when we finished, so PH and Hao-Chien simul-rapped for efficiency.

PH leading SW Corner.

It was almost fully dark when we came down. Another group was just starting up, and we got a photo of them carrying colored lights to the top for a party.

D6 12/27

We had to take Y to the airport in the afternoon, so we only had the morning to climb. Decided to climb at Intersection Rock near the Hidden Valley Campground parking lot.

  • Overhang Bypass (5.7, 2 pitches): P1 PH led. You climb out of a big scoop to underneath the roof. MP comments say going around to the left is easier, but PH missed that and went straight up, which is harder. The crack is super narrow at the start (#.1), with a sidepull jug above that is a bit far. P2 Hao-Chien led. The traverse is a little like 踢刀邊緣, but shorter, with some footholds below that you can cheat with. Overall it honestly did not feel as fun as 剃刀. The summit anchor is shared by many routes, so be prepared to coordinate with other parties.

The scoop on P1 of Overhang Bypass. The easier way should be the crack on the left.

The traverse on P2 of Overhang Bypass. There are some footholds below that you can cheat with.

On D1 we saw someone doing aerial yoga on the platform at the top of Overhang Bypass P1.

D7 12/28

Started today with routes in Conan's Corridor at Jumbo Rock. Once the approach leaves the trail there are quite a lot of plants, then it enters a very narrow canyon. When it opens up again, you are there. The Jumbo Rock Campground parking lot only has four spaces. We got lucky and found one; otherwise you have to park out on the roadside.

The canyon on the approach to Conan's Corridor.

  • Colorado Crack (5.9): PH led. Hand crack, finger crack, thin hands, all of it. Five-star route and genuinely excellent. In a few spots you can even lean your body against the wall and go no-hands. Felt like Longdong 5.10b.

PH leading Colorado Crack.

  • Spiderman (5.10b): After the previous route, we set up a TR solo from above. The lower crack is irregular and feels a little awkward to protect. Higher up it widens into off-width. A #4 is enough at the very top (we re-fixed the rope here to avoid abrasion).

PH TR-soloing Spiderman.

After that we went back to Echo Rock planning to climb Touch and Go, but another party of three beat us to it. We were worried Pope's Crack would take too long, so we drove back to Overbolted Rock at Split Rock for a short sport route.

  • Boulevard of Dreams (5.10a): PH tried to lead but could not even get off the ground, so Hao-Chien had to lead it. Friction slab starting from a big boulder, stepping on a bunch of imaginary footholds. By the time PH top-roped it, it was already dark. Even with a headlamp, the holds were hard to see (battery was dying).

Boulevard of Dreams at night. Start from the big boulder on the right.

D8 12/29

Today we climbed at Emerald City in the OZ area. Drive to the trailhead shown on the MP map; there is roadside parking. DO NOT TRUST THE TRAIL ON MP. Just use GPS and walk roughly in the direction of the trail. Sometimes you find a use trail, follow it for a while, and then realize it has drifted away from the line on the MP map again. Until somebody builds a real trail, you may just have to bushwhack with your phone in hand. Following the general direction of the MP trail takes you around a few hills and then into a dry wash full of huge boulders. Emerald City is behind the wall on river right (left side when facing upstream). The map initially crosses around from river left and then back to river right, but I think you can probably just stay on river right and slowly work your way up wherever possible (there is absolutely no trail here anymore). Total approach was about an hour. Bring enough water; the desert is very dry. We also saw a rattlesnake in a crack along the way, so watch out. Near the top the rocks start turning kind of green (maybe that is where the area name came from?).

The desert in the first part of the OZ Area approach. Basically stare at your phone and cross this.

The dry wash toward Emerald City. Emerald City is behind the sunlit wall in the upper left.

Rattlesnake we saw near Emerald City. It really does make a sound like this (though we never actually saw its tail). After that we were both a little nervous and watched our steps much more carefully.

  • The Rattler (5.10c): PH led. Starts with finger crack into thin hands, overhanging but with a very unusual corner. It reminded us of Clam Digger at Liming: you brace with your knee and the sole of your foot. After a ledge it finishes with an OW somewhat like 龍路. Felt like Longdong 5.11.

The move out of the corner on The Rattler. Felt like the crux.

  • Snake Book (5.11b): After the previous route we set up a TR directly from above. Stem the corner all the way to the top; sometimes the crack can help with shifting your weight.

The corner on Snake Book.

We originally wanted to climb the arete in the Hollywood Area afterward, but by the time we walked back it was almost dark, so we gave up on that.

Unfortunately, by the time I linked the first part everyone was getting ready to leave, so I did not have time to finish working the crux and link the whole thing. The holds are good from start to finish; it is just a little burly and fairly long. Good for people with weak fingers (like me). Would try again if I come back.

Afterthoughts

Hao-Chien

We climbed all kinds of cracks on this trip, big and small, from finger cracks all the way to off-width, so I got to practice a lot of different techniques. I especially got much more confident with finger jams. Off-width was basically new to me, and I still do not really know what I am doing. It is insanely tiring and painful. My newer pair of shoes also happened to be worn through right before this trip, so the only stiff shoes I had left were my freshly resoled Instinct VS. Because they are downsized, jamming my feet into cracks hurt like hell; the outside has no rubber so it felt like I was destroying the shoes; and there is no ankle protection, so whenever I braced my ankle in an off-width I just ground it against the rock. I guess there is actually a reason the TC Pro covers your ankles.

Another highlight was “Know your system.” Over these 10 days we used a bunch of different systems. Besides the basic stuff we use most often at 中岩, depending on the situation we also used TR solo, simul rap, simul climb, belaying with a Pinch or Spoc, and various ways of extending anchors with the rope (to avoid rope drag/abrasion, improve efficiency, etc.). It really showed that outdoors, learning one single procedure and trying to use it for every situation does not work very well. You need to understand the pros and cons of different methods so you can pick one based on the situation and what you care about at the time.

This was basically my second time outdoor bouldering. Before the trip I had delusions of climbing some really hard stuff, but in reality my strength on small crimps (<10 mm) is still terrible. Need more training. Also, if one or two people want to try harder problems (usually long, big moves, or both), you either need one person to own a ridiculous number of pads or you need to go with a group. For example, we met a pair trying Thin Lizzy and the two of them had brought four pads. At Caveman there were several groups rotating through, and the cave had at least 7-8 pads in it.

Climbing 10 days in a row sounds exhausting, but looking back it honestly was not that bad. It mostly depends on what you climb. For the first eight days we did not climb that much each day, so aside from my shoulders getting a little tight, I was not actually that tired. The shoulders were mostly from belaying from above: after a few days with the Giga Jul I got tired of it and switched to the Pinch, which helped a bit. If you want even less effort, a progress-capture pulley is another option, though you lose some flexibility in feeding rope. D9 was different. After a full day of bouldering, I woke up the next morning feeling like I had been hit by a car, and my palms felt like they were on fire. Even now, two days after getting home, my hands still feel like there is a layer of film over them and typing feels awful XD. Of course, one major reason I survived is that I did not lead any of the off-widths XD (thanks PH). Anyway, if you plan the climbing sensibly, climbing many days in a row seems pretty manageable. Thinking back, Red Rock last year was similar: only D1 and D7 had harder sport climbing.

Thanks to PH for doing another 10 days of dirtbagging with me one year later, and for leading a bunch of fun routes for me to climb. On lots of trad routes my mindset was basically “if I cannot lead it, I can always come down and make PH lead it XD.” Unfortunately PH already has plans for next Christmas, so: now recruiting a partner for next Christmas XD. Also thanks to Y for trusting us enough to come climb with us.

First, I have to thank Hao-Chien for being willing to climb at Joshua Tree with me. We had actually planned to go bouldering at Hueco Tanks in Texas, and had almost finished arranging the car, accommodation, and guide. Then in mid-October I ruptured the A2 pulley in my middle finger. Even moderate bouldering was too much. After a lot of rest and rehab I could still only climb routes that did not require crimping, so we ended up choosing Joshua Tree, famous for cracks and slabs.

Most of the Joshua Tree trad routes we climbed were cracks. Personally, the difficulty was way beyond what I expected. It was not just Joshua Tree's famous sandbagging. The routes are generally long, I am not very familiar with crack techniques other than hand cracks, and on top of that I forgot the nut set on this trip... which limited our protection options a bit. Most of the routes we climbed protected well, but the same crack size often repeats over and over, so every time I placed gear I was slightly worried: “I'm out of .75s already. What am I going to do later?” I had to think about gear management constantly. The rack suggestions in the guidebook can also be pretty brief, and MP often does not describe things that precisely. I also realized my trad head still is not strong enough. I place gear too close together, and maybe the finger injury made me even more risk-averse mentally. I finally got to climb OW again too. You summon every muscle in your body, grind yourself against the rock, and desperately inch upward, only to come away with cuts and bruises all over your knees, ankles, and elbows. Sometimes the giant cams hanging from your harness get jammed and you literally cannot move. Somehow, looking back afterward, all that torture is still fun to think about. We got to climb three OWs on this trip. Thanks to my rope partners for suffering with me. In short, Joshua Tree's cracks taught me a lesson.

The slab was also way beyond what I expected. Maybe I brought shoes with too little support (MadRock Drone), but after barely a few moves the bottoms of my feet were always sore... I kind of regret bringing shoes this soft. On those imaginary friction-slab footholds I could barely feel anything. Thinking back, the shoes I used for friction climbing before were all fairly stiff (e.g. Miura, Otaki). For the crazy slab routes on this trip I basically relied on Hao-Chien. While hanging on “Boulevard of Dreams,” I probably praised his footwork and his mental game on lead more than 100 times. Then while seconding “Heart and Sole,” I could not understand how anyone could still have that much faith in slab hands and feet while it was raining. Anyway, I have to give it to Hao-Chien when it comes to friction slab.

Besides climbing, way too much happened during these 10 days in Southern California: a sandstorm almost blew our tent away; we drove on flooded roads; plant thorns stabbed through a shoe; every day started or ended with discounted large fries and coffee from McDonald's; we tried grilling salmon on aluminum foil and failed miserably (the pan-fried steak was successful though); discovered corn grilled over charcoal is really good; went to visitor centers to read the free guidebooks; did lots of fun scrambling; got mildly traumatized by California gas prices; became afraid every crack might contain a rattlesnake; felt incredibly grateful for electrical outlets in public parks; collected other people's leftover firewood everywhere to make fires; and eventually saw so many Joshua trees that we became numb to the desert scenery... Ten days of dirtbag climbing was still pretty brutal. We did not climb on Day 2 and barely climbed on Day 6, but by the end of Day 7 my entire body was sore. Day 8, after getting tortured by OW and stemming routes, I could barely walk normally out of the trailhead. On Day 9 we bouldered on too many rough slopers and it felt like several layers of skin had disappeared from my palms. When I woke up on Day 10, my whole body was refusing to get on the wall. Quietly, I started feeling like this might be my last trip dirtbagging this hard. Apparently I am still kind of delicate and need a soft bed to properly rest and recover. Maybe I cannot stretch trips to 10 days anymore, or I need real rest days in the middle, or we need to climb easier routes. But most of the classic fun routes are not that easy... No idea if I will ever get another chance to come here and escape winter. Finally, I hope Hao-Chien finds someone to climb with next Christmas.
